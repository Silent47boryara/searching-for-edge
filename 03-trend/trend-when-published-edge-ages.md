# 03 — Trend: When Published Edge Ages

**Author:** Oleg Arefev
**Published:** August 2026
**Status:** `Published`
**Class:** Literature-based (no own Binance replication — closed at the literature stage on two independent grounds, statistical and retail-feasibility; see Section 7)

**Research question:** does time-series momentum — one of the most repeatedly confirmed strategies in traditional-asset finance — still hold as a real edge in cryptocurrency markets today, or did the early, favorable crypto evidence describe a market regime that has since passed? And, separately from whether the effect is statistically real anywhere in the data: where the literature does show a positive number, is that number something a retail trader, on a real exchange, with real capital, could actually reach — or does reaching it require a universe, or a moment in time, retail doesn't have access to?

---

## 1. Why Trend Looked Like the Safe Bet

Of the strategies this series has tested so far, trend-following (in its academic form, time-series momentum) came into this project with the strongest pedigree by far. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen (2012), in "Time Series Momentum," tested the strategy across 58 liquid futures and forward contracts — equity indices, currencies, commodities, and government bonds — over more than two decades (1985–2009), and found the effect present in nearly every asset class they examined, not concentrated in one corner of the data.

That combination — a simple, economically intuitive mechanism (an asset that has been rising keeps rising, on average, over the following weeks to months) confirmed across unrelated asset classes and a long sample — is close to the best case a discretionary strategy can make for itself in the academic literature. It's a large part of why trend-following managed futures funds exist as a real, decades-old segment of the asset management industry, not just a backtest curiosity. Coming into this series, trend was the strategy expected to survive the crypto test most easily.

## 2. This Series' Standing Question: Not Just "Is It Real," But "Can Retail Reach It"

Every note in this series runs the same two checks, and it's worth stating both explicitly before going further, because this note leans on the second one more heavily than the previous two did.

The first check is the one academic peer review already does: is the effect statistically real, in a sample and methodology serious enough to trust. The second check is one that a published paper has no obligation to answer, because it isn't the paper's job: even where the first check passes, can an individual with a retail-sized account, on a real exchange, using a realistic execution style, actually harvest the number in the paper — or does reaching it quietly require a universe, a fee tier, or a moment in market history that retail doesn't have?

The previous two notes in this series each found a different way for the second check to fail. In the Grid note (Part I), the failure was structural: the paper's strongest reported result for the delta-neutral variant assumed a maker-rebate fee tier — Binance's own Futures Market Maker Program requires an application and roughly 1,000 BTC of 30-day trading volume before it grants negative maker fees — that a standard retail account, at any point in time, simply cannot get approved for, regardless of skill or capital deployed on a single account. In the Momentum note (Part II), the failure showed up only after we ran the numbers ourselves: the published cross-sectional effect was real enough on paper, but on our own Binance replication, realistic execution costs consumed about 70% of an already-marginal weekly spread, leaving nothing distinguishable from zero.

This note adds a third, distinct failure mode, and it shows up twice inside the same paper. First: one of the two candidate implementations of time-series momentum in the crypto literature — Grobys et al.'s broader, 2,500-coin robustness check (Section 5 below) — is not a universe a retail trader can access on a single exchange to begin with, since the overwhelming majority of those 2,500 coins never had, and still don't have, a liquid Binance perpetual to trade either leg on. Second, and more specific to trend as a category: where the literature's own numbers are positive at all, they belong overwhelmingly to a market regime — pre-July-2020 crypto — that no longer exists and that a trader today cannot go back and open a position in. An edge that is real only in the past isn't a retail-infrastructure problem in the usual sense (no fee tier or account size fixes it), but it is a retail-access problem in exactly the sense this section is about: the paper's positive number and the market a retail trader can actually trade today are not the same thing.

## 3. What Time-Series Momentum Actually Measures

It's worth being precise about the distinction from the cross-sectional momentum tested in this series' previous note (Part II). Cross-sectional momentum ranks assets against each other and bets that recent relative winners keep outperforming recent relative losers — the portfolio is long winners and short losers regardless of whether the overall market is up or down. Time-series momentum instead looks at each asset's own recent trailing return in isolation: if an asset's own price has risen over the lookback window, go long it; if it has fallen, go short it (or, in a long-only implementation, simply stay out). The comparison is an asset against its own past, not against its peers.

This matters for what a "trend" strategy is actually claiming: it isn't betting that winners beat losers, it's betting that recent direction persists into the near future — a bet on momentum in the trend itself, closer to what most retail traders mean when they say "the trend is your friend" than the more academic cross-sectional-ranking form of momentum.

## 4. The Early Crypto Evidence

Liu and Tsyvinski (2021, *The Review of Financial Studies*), in "Risks and Returns of Cryptocurrency," found a strong time-series momentum effect for Bitcoin: a one-standard-deviation increase in a given week's return was associated with increases of 3.16%, 3.66%, 3.49%, and 1.50% in returns one, two, three, and four weeks ahead, respectively — a result the authors report as statistically significant across those horizons.

The detail that matters most for this note is the sample window. The paper's Bitcoin sample runs to May 2018, and its Ripple and Ethereum samples end on the same date. This was a market that looked very different from crypto today: no regulated, deeply liquid USDⓈ-M perpetual futures market on an exchange the size of Binance, minimal institutional participation, and none of the post-2020 structural changes (the 2020–2021 bull cycle, the 2022 collapses, and the subsequent maturation of derivatives markets) that later data would need to be tested against. A strong result on pre-2018 data is real evidence — but it's evidence about a market regime, not a permanent property of crypto markets. It's also, mechanically, evidence from a period during which the retail execution infrastructure this series otherwise assumes (deep, broad-coverage Binance perpetuals, both long and short, across dozens of large-cap alts at once) was in its infancy or didn't exist yet — a point Section 7 returns to directly.

## 5. The 2025 Test: Does the Effect Survive a Longer, More Recent Sample?

Grobys, Kolari, Sandretto, Shahzad, and Äijö (2025), in "Cryptocurrency Momentum Has (Not) Its Moments" (*Financial Markets and Portfolio Management*, 39, 443–476), is the most recent and, on sample length and methodology, the strongest available crypto-specific test of this question. The study uses 30 large-cap cryptocurrencies over 416 weekly observations, January 2016 through December 2023 — a sample roughly five and a half years longer than Liu and Tsyvinski's, and one that spans both the older, pre-2018 regime and the market's post-2020 structure.

The headline time-series momentum results, split by regime:

- **Full sample (2016–2023):** 0.90% per week, not statistically significant.
- **Pre-July 2020:** 1.74% per week, significant only at the 10% level.
- **Post-July 2020 (the regime closest to today's market):** negative, and not statistically significant.

In other words: on the older half of the sample — the regime closest to what Liu and Tsyvinski tested — there's a positive point estimate, though only marginally significant even there. On the newer half, the point estimate flips negative and loses any significance. The paper's own abstract summarizes the broader finding directly: cryptocurrency momentum "is subject to severe crashes," and the authors note that individual cryptocurrencies can be extreme enough to render the whole portfolio's returns statistically insignificant. Consistent with that, the authors report that after trimming the single largest outlier from the sample, the same time-series momentum return recovers to 1.51% per week with a t-statistic of 2.63 — significant at the 1% level. That contrast between the raw and the outlier-trimmed result is itself the finding: a small number of extreme, idiosyncratic crypto crashes are doing enough work in the raw sample to erase statistical significance almost entirely.

The paper also reports that volatility-managed (volatility-scaled) momentum portfolios substantially improve on the raw strategy — more than doubling the raw payoff and reaching 1.86% per week (significant at the 5% level) using an 8-week rolling volatility window — and frames this as consistent with the equity-momentum literature, where volatility scaling is a known way to manage momentum crash risk. Separately, the authors estimate a power-law tail exponent of roughly α ≈ 3 for the return distribution, which implies the underlying variance is not well-defined in the classical sense — a heavy-tailed regime closer to venture-capital-style return distributions than to the roughly normal returns assumed by standard significance tests.

The authors trace the mechanism behind that outlier-driven insignificance to a single episode: the cryptocurrency Mindol (MIN) rose from $0.54 to $7.86 on December 23, 2020 — a one-day move of roughly 1,400% — which, sitting in the short leg of a six-cryptocurrency portfolio, produced a single week's momentum-strategy loss of approximately −255%. The authors state directly that this one coin's move accounts for the raw strategy's insignificant payoff, which is exactly why the outlier-trimmed result (1.51% per week, significant at 1%, above) looks so different from the raw one. As the authors put it, this crash mechanism is "substantially different from the optionality effect documented in the equity market" — the crash risk here doesn't come from a diversified basket behaving like equity index options near market turns, it comes from single, idiosyncratic coin-level events extreme enough to move a whole portfolio's weekly return by over two hundred percent.

The paper also runs a separate robustness check on a much larger, value-weighted universe of 2,500 coins rather than the 30 large-cap coins used in the main analysis. There, the result is worse, not better: the value-weighted momentum factor on this broader universe is statistically significantly negative at −3.40% per week over the full 2016–2023 sample, and — importantly — this negative result survives multiple trimming approaches, with average payoffs remaining significantly negative (ranging from −1.18% to −2.69% per week) even after removing the most extreme observations. In other words, the crash risk documented above for the 30-coin sample isn't a quirk of a small basket that a bigger, more diversified universe fixes — the effect gets more negative, not less, once the universe is widened.

## 6. Why Volatility Scaling Doesn't Change the Verdict Here

It would be tempting to read the volatility-managed result — 1.86% per week, significant at 5% — as the strategy being rescued. It's worth being precise about what that result actually shows and doesn't show.

Volatility scaling changes position sizing in response to recent volatility; it doesn't change the underlying return-generating mechanism. If the base time-series momentum effect in the post-2020 regime is negative and insignificant — as the split-sample result above shows — then a volatility overlay is being layered onto a base effect that, in the regime that matters for a trader today, isn't there to begin with. The paper's own headline volatility-scaled figure is reported for the full 2016–2023 sample, which is still weighted toward the older, pre-2020 regime where a marginal (10%-significant) effect existed. It does not establish that volatility-scaled time-series momentum is a real edge specifically in the current market regime — that would require the same split-sample test applied to the volatility-managed version, which is not something we've verified this paper reports.

More generally, and independent of this specific study: a risk-management overlay like volatility scaling can reasonably be expected to improve an already-existing, real edge by smoothing out crash risk and improving a Sharpe ratio. It is a different, much stronger claim to say the same overlay can create an edge out of a base signal that isn't statistically distinguishable from zero in the first place. The paper's own robustness check makes this point directly, without us needing to speculate: on the broader, value-weighted 2,500-coin universe (Section 5), the raw momentum factor is significantly negative, and the authors report that after applying the same volatility-scaling risk management, average payoffs remain statistically significantly negative — the crashes are removed, but the sign and significance of the underlying loss are not. Risk management there smooths the ride without curing the underlying problem; only on the narrower, 30-coin, equal-weighted construction does volatility scaling turn a marginal, regime-dependent positive into a clearly significant one. Reading the favorable version of that result in isolation, without checking whether it holds under the paper's own alternative construction, is exactly the kind of after-the-fact rescue this series' methodology (see the repository README) is built to catch.

## 7. The Decision to Close Without a Binance Replication: Two Independent Grounds

Every other note in this series so far has followed literature review with an independent replication on Binance data, priced against realistic trading costs. This note stops one step earlier, and it's worth being transparent about why — on two separate grounds, either one of which would be sufficient on its own.

### 7.1 The statistical ground

This project's own working rule, stated plainly: read the literature first, and if there is no fundamental shift in the evidence that would justify expecting a different outcome on Binance data, close the hypothesis at the literature stage rather than spending the time on reverse-engineering and a net-of-costs backtest that the evidence already argues will fail its first, most basic hurdle — is the effect statistically real in the regime that matters. For cross-sectional momentum (Part II of this series), the literature was genuinely split enough, and multiple specifications different enough, that running our own numbers was the only way to get an answer. For time-series momentum in crypto, the most recent, longest, and methodologically strongest available study already answers that first question in the regime we'd actually be trading: not present, not close to significant, and the closest thing to a positive number is generated by a data window that predates the market structure we operate in today.

### 7.2 The retail-feasibility ground — applied even to the best-case published numbers

Section 2 set up the second, independent check this series applies: even where a number in the literature is positive, can a retail trader actually reach it. Applied here, on the two candidate universes the literature offers:

The broad, 2,500-coin universe is not one a retail trader can access to begin with — and the published number there is worse anyway. Grobys et al.'s robustness check spans 2,500 coins, value-weighted, rebalanced weekly. The overwhelming majority of those names never had, and still don't have, a liquid perpetual futures market on Binance or any comparably regulated venue — Binance's own futures product currently lists well under a thousand pairs in total, a small fraction of 2,500, and coverage was narrower still for most of the 2016–2023 sample period, since Binance's futures product itself only launched in 2019 — meaning a retail trader cannot open the short leg (in many cases, cannot open either leg with real depth) for most of that universe on a single, realistic execution venue. This is the same class of problem this series already documented for grid trading's institutional fee tier (Part I): a published number that depends on access retail doesn't have. Here it's compounded rather than offset by the numbers themselves: the 2,500-coin result is significantly negative (−3.40%/week) and stays significantly negative under every trimming approach the authors test (−1.18% to −2.69%/week). There is no version of "if only I could access the broader universe" that rescues this note — the broader universe is both harder to access and worse on paper.

The narrower, 30-coin universe is accessible on Binance today — but its positive number belongs to a market that no longer exists. Unlike the 2,500-coin case, 30 large-cap coins with liquid Binance perpetuals on both sides is a genuinely tradable universe for a retail account right now. That makes the failure mode here more specific, and worth stating precisely: the positive, even if only marginally significant, point estimate (1.74%/week pre-July-2020; 1.51%/week after trimming the largest outlier; 1.86%/week under volatility scaling) is a description of a regime — January 2016 through July 2020 — that a trader today cannot go back and open a position in. It isn't a fee tier or an account-size problem, the kind of retail obstacle a bigger deposit or a better broker fixes. It's a calendar problem. The only regime a retail trader can trade right now is the post-July-2020 one, and in that regime the same paper's own point estimate is negative and not statistically significant. Whatever positive edge existed in the earlier data, a retail trader logging in today is not being offered a seat at that table.

It's also worth being honest about scale, rather than reaching for a false precision this project doesn't have: our own cross-sectional Binance replication (Part II) found that realistic execution costs — a Binance taker fee plus a modest slippage assumption, applied to a weekly-rebalanced, long-short crypto basket — consumed about 70% of an already-marginal +0.573%/week gross spread, leaving nothing distinguishable from zero. Time-series momentum's best-case published numbers (1.51–1.86%/week) are larger than that cross-sectional headline, so we are not claiming the same cost erosion would necessarily erase them the same way — that would be a specific quantitative claim about a strategy we have not run ourselves, and this project doesn't publish numbers it hasn't checked. What that earlier result does establish, honestly, is the order of magnitude retail execution costs take out of a weekly-rebalanced crypto basket strategy on Binance — context worth keeping in mind, not a substitute for a test we didn't run, and not needed here anyway, since the regime-lock argument above is sufficient on its own.

Running our own Binance replication of the 30-coin construction would mean re-deriving, at real cost in time and compute, a result the existing literature has already established with more data than would be practical to reproduce independently at this project's scale — and doing so on a regime (today's market) where the same authors' own split-sample test already reports no effect to find. That isn't laziness — it's the same discipline that led this series to publish two closed, negative-adjacent hypotheses in a row rather than force a third variant "to save it." A closed hypothesis is still a result.

## 8. Verdict

Time-series momentum has genuine academic standing — Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen's original result, across dozens of markets and decades of data, is one of the more robust cross-asset findings in the momentum literature, and there's nothing wrong with the mechanism as tested on traditional assets. The crypto-specific evidence, however, tells a regime-dependent story: an early, only marginally significant positive result on pre-2018 data (Liu and Tsyvinski), followed by a longer, more recent, more careful test (Grobys, Kolari, Sandretto, Shahzad, and Äijö) that finds the effect statistically indistinguishable from zero in the regime closest to today's market — and negative in point estimate. The raw strategy's apparent viability, where it exists at all, appears concentrated in a small number of extreme, fat-tailed episodes rather than in a broad, reliable drift, and a volatility-scaling overlay that improves the full-sample number doesn't, on the evidence available to us, establish that the base effect exists in the current regime for that overlay to improve.

This note closes on two independent grounds, not one. Statistically, the effect is not established in the regime that matters — that alone would be enough to close it, as it was for cross-sectional momentum in Part II. Separately, and just as sufficient on its own: where the literature's numbers are positive at all, they describe either a universe retail cannot access (the 2,500-coin case, which is also negative on the numbers) or a calendar window retail cannot re-enter (the 30-coin case, pre-July-2020). This is a case of a published edge that appears to have aged: real, or close to real, in an earlier data window, and not established as real — nor reachable — in the current one. That is a different and more specific claim than "trend-following doesn't work" — it's a claim about this specific published evidence, on Binance-comparable crypto markets, at this specific point in time, for the specific trader reading this now.

## 9. What This Means for a Retail Trader

If you're running, or considering running, a simple time-series trend system on crypto today — long when recent price action is up, flat or short when it's down — the honest summary of the evidence is: the most recent and most careful academic test of exactly this idea, on a multi-year sample that includes the current market structure, does not find a statistically reliable edge in the period that matters. What positive-looking result exists in the literature comes from an older regime and from a small number of extreme episodes, not from a broad, dependable drift you could expect to harvest systematically going forward.

This note is also a useful contrast with the other two closed hypotheses in this series so far, because the failure mode is different in kind. Grid's problem (Part I) was a fee tier — in principle fixable with enough capital or the right account, just not at retail scale. Cross-sectional momentum's problem (Part II) was costs eating a real but marginal edge — in principle fixable with lower fees or a larger edge, even if neither was available to us. Trend's problem, for the one universe that's actually accessible on Binance today, isn't fixable by any amount of capital, any fee negotiation, or any infrastructure improvement: the evidence that looks positive belongs to a period of market history that has already happened. No account size buys you a time machine back to 2019.

None of this means every discretionary or systematic trend-following approach is worthless — this note tests one specific, published academic formulation of time-series momentum, not the entire universe of ways a trader might define and trade "trend." But it does mean that citing academic trend-following's strong track record on traditional assets, by itself, is not sufficient evidence that the same mechanism is currently a tradable edge on crypto — the literature's own most recent and careful answer, for this specific question, is closer to "not established, and not currently reachable even where it once was" than to "confirmed."
